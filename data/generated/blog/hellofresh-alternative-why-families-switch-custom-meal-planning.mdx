Meal kits were supposed to solve dinner. And for a while — maybe the first few weeks — they kind of did. Then the boxes started piling up, the portions were off for your family of five, and you were still standing in the grocery store on Wednesday because the kit didn't include anything for lunch, snacks, or that one night you always order pizza anyway.

Here's what most meal kit comparisons don't tell you: the problem isn't which meal kit service you chose. It's that the meal kit model has a ceiling. There are things it structurally cannot do, no matter how many cuisine options they add to the menu. If dinner still feels like a scramble, these five limitations are probably why.

---

## 1. A Meal Kit Can't Learn That Your Kid Refuses Anything Green

Personalization in the meal kit world means picking from a curated menu of 40 or so options. That's not nothing — but it's also not the same as a system that actually knows your household. It doesn't know that your 7-year-old will eat broccoli only if it's roasted and slightly crispy, that your partner keeps halal, or that you've been trying to work more anti-inflammatory ingredients into the rotation since January.

What you get instead is a weekly menu designed for a hypothetical average family. You filter out the obvious mismatches, pick the least-bad options, and hope for the best. After trying both approaches, I'd describe the meal kit experience as "choosing from someone else's meal plan" rather than actually planning meals.

A real meal kit alternative builds the plan around your household's actual restrictions, allergies, and preferences — not a dropdown of 40 pre-approved recipes.

---

## 2. Meal Kits Won't Use What's Already in Your Fridge

This one is quietly expensive. You've got half a bag of spinach, four chicken thighs from last week's Costco run, and a can of coconut milk you bought for a recipe you never made. A meal kit sends you more ingredients on top of all that. The spinach wilts. The chicken thighs get pushed to the back. You throw away $15 worth of food and feel vaguely annoyed about it.

Custom meal planning — done well — works the other way around. You tell it what you have, and it builds the week around those ingredients first. That's not a minor convenience. It changes the whole relationship between your grocery list and your kitchen.

---

## 3. They Can't Adjust a Recipe Mid-Week Without Charging You

Say the salmon dish you planned for Thursday looks like a 45-minute production and it's already 6:15 PM. With a meal kit, your options are: make it anyway, or abandon the kit and order takeout. There's no "make this faster" button. No "swap Thursday and Saturday." No "actually skip this one and give me something else."

The rigidity is baked into the model. Meal kits are physical products — once the box ships, the menu is locked.

A good meal kit alternative lets you modify on the fly. Swap meals between days. Regenerate a single night without redoing the whole week. Ask for a faster version of what you had planned. That kind of flexibility is what actually gets dinner on the table on a Thursday in March.

## 4. A Meal Kit Can't Scale Correctly for Your Family Size

Meal kits typically offer 2-serving or 4-serving options. If you have three kids and two adults, you're either ordering the 4-serving box and hoping it stretches, or you're ordering two boxes and paying accordingly. Neither is clean.

Families of six, seven, or eight are essentially unsupported. And even at four servings, the portions assume adults — not a mix of a 4-year-old, a 9-year-old, a teenager with a hollow leg, and two grown-ups with different appetites.

Automatic recipe scaling to your actual household size sounds like a small thing until you've spent a year doing the math yourself every time you cook.

---

## 5. They Can't Involve Your Family in the Decision

"What do you want for dinner?" is a question that starts arguments. Meal kits solve it by removing everyone's input entirely — you pick from the menu, the box arrives, that's what you're having. For some families that's fine. For others, getting buy-in from the people who have to eat the food is half the battle.

There's a middle ground between full democracy (chaos) and top-down meal assignment (resentment). A voting-based approach — where family members swipe on recipe options and the most popular picks land in the weekly plan — sounds gimmicky until you try it with a 10-year-old who suddenly has opinions about Korean BBQ. Turns out kids eat food they voted for. Who knew.

---

None of this is to say meal kits are useless. For a household of two with no dietary restrictions and a flexible grocery budget, they work fine. But for most families — with picky eaters, dietary needs, varying schedules, and real kitchens full of half-used ingredients — the meal kit model hits a wall pretty fast.

What actually works is a planning system that knows your household, respects your constraints, and bends when life doesn't go according to plan. That's the difference between a meal kit and a genuine meal kit alternative — one ships you a box, the other helps you figure out dinner.
